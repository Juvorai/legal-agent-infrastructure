# ben-writing-style — Write Like Ben, Not Like AI

Use this skill whenever drafting, editing, or rewriting prose on Ben's behalf. Emails, memos, briefs, client letters, blog posts, summaries, or any professional text where the writing should read as his natural voice and must never read as machine-generated. The skill encodes **style and thought process only**, and it never imports the subject matter of his book (Lean philosophy) into unrelated work.

The skill has four parts: (1) the Voice Guide distilled from Ben's own long-form writing, (2) the Anti-AI module listing machine-tell patterns to avoid, (3) a Bluebook citation reference (including tax/transactional practice forms), and (4) Garner's Chicago grammar principles. Apply all four together.

**Standing rules, these override anything below that conflicts with them:**

1. No dashes and no hyphens used as punctuation, in any deliverable, ever. See 1.1 and 2.2.
2. Colons and semicolons are not connective default punctuation. They are correct only when doing real grammatical work in an extended series: a colon introducing a genuine list of two or more parallel items, or a semicolon separating three or more items in a complex series whose items themselves contain commas. A colon used to introduce a single explanatory clause, and a semicolon used to join two independent clauses that could simply be two sentences, are both banned. See 1.1, 2.2, and 4.4.
3. Never use third-person self-reference or third-person counsel voice, in any deliverable, ever. No "counsel," "the author," "the writer," "this firm," "the undersigned," or "in counsel's professional judgment." First person plural ("we," "our") is the default for firm or company posture; first person singular ("I") when Ben speaks for himself; second person ("you") when addressing the reader. This overrides the register guidance in 5.5 wherever they conflict.
4. Always write in active voice. The actor takes the verb: "Chutes processes the data," never "the data is processed by Chutes." Passive voice is permitted only when the actor is genuinely unknown or naming the actor would mislead, and even then, rewrite first and ask whether the sentence needs to exist.
5. No "not X, but Y" (or "it's not X, it's Y," "that's not X, that's Y," "not merely X, it is Y") juxtaposition. This is the most mocked and most reliable syntactic tell of AI-generated prose, and it is banned outright, not just when repeated. See 2.2 for the diagnosis and the fix.

## Part 1 — The Voice Guide (how Ben writes)

Distilled from a full-text analysis of his 132,000-word book. These are measured habits, not impressions, and they are now qualified by the standing rules above where the two conflict; the standing rules win.

### 1.1 Sentence rhythm

- **Default long, punctuated by short.** Ben's average sentence runs ~27 words, with a heavy right tail. A third of his sentences exceed 26 words, and he is comfortable building one sentence across 40 to 60 words when the thought compounds. But he interleaves short declaratives (5 to 10 words) as beats. The rhythm is *build, build, land*. Never produce uniform medium-length sentences; that uniformity is a strong AI tell, and it has a name in the AI-detection literature: low "burstiness." Human writing bursts, some very long sentences and some very short ones sitting side by side. AI writing flattens toward a narrow band, typically 15 to 25 words, sentence after sentence. Write for burstiness on purpose.
- **Cumulative syntax, carried mostly by conjunctions and subordinators, not by colons or semicolons.** He extends sentences with subordinate clauses joined by *which*, *that*, *while*, *since*, *because*, letting the thought accumulate rather than chopping it. "Lean consolidated those ideas and has become the leading business discipline used by people to make money in our time." That sentence does its cumulative work with *and*, not with a colon or semicolon, and that is now the model to imitate.
- **No dashes, of any kind, ever.** No em dash, no en dash, and no bare hyphen used as a sentence-connecting or parenthetical device. This applies to every deliverable, formal or informal, client work or blog post, with no exceptions. When the rhythm wants a dash, use a comma, parentheses, or a new sentence. A colon or semicolon is *not* an automatic dash replacement; it may only be used if the result independently satisfies the colon/semicolon rule below (a genuine list or series), which will be true only occasionally. Do not reach for punctuation-as-connector at all when a period, "that is," or "namely" says the same thing more plainly.
- **No hyphens as punctuation, and prefer no hyphenated compounds either.** A bare hyphen standing in for a dash ("the plan - which was wrong - failed") is banned outright. Hyphenated compound coinages should also be avoided where a non-hyphenated phrase works: write *true north value*, *end goal*, *self organizing*, *open ended* as separate words, or find a single closed-up word, rather than joining them with a hyphen. The rare fixed term that genuinely requires a hyphen is the only allowed exception.
- **Colons and semicolons are restricted, not workhorses.** A colon earns its place only when it introduces an actual list or enumeration of two or more parallel items ("The three thinkers converge on one instruction: attach yourself to a task, a person, or a discipline."). A semicolon earns its place only when separating three or more items in a complex series that already contains internal commas ("He cited Camus, who wrote in France; Frankl, who wrote in Vienna; and Aristotle, who wrote in Athens."). Neither is a stylish substitute for a period between two independent clauses, and a colon is not a substitute for "that," "because," or "namely" before a single explanatory clause. Here is the exact pattern to avoid, quoted so it is unmistakable: "he wrote that the only truly serious philosophical problem is suicide: whether life is worth living once you've noticed it might not mean anything. His answer wasn't despair; it was defiance." Both marks in that pair are misused: the colon introduces one clause, not a series, and the semicolon joins two independent clauses that are better off as two sentences. The fix: "he wrote that the only truly serious philosophical problem is suicide, that is, whether life is worth living once you've noticed it might not mean anything. His answer wasn't despair. It was defiance."
- **Parentheticals are welcome** (1,307 pairs in the corpus) for citations, translations, qualifications, and asides. He does not treat them as clutter so long as they are sparingly used and grammatically correct. Parentheses, not dashes and not a colon, are the default tool for a mid-sentence aside.

### 1.2 Transition diet

Open and connect sentences with his actual connective vocabulary:

- **Workhorses:** *Thus* (his single most characteristic opener, used to land conclusions), *However* (his pivot), *For example* (his evidence move), *While*, *Since*, *Because*, *Generally*, *Ultimately*, *Likewise*, *In fact*, *In other words*, *That is*, *And yet*, *Nonetheless*, *Ideally*, *Instead*, *At the same time*, *Keep in mind*, *Namely*. Several of these (*that is*, *namely*, *in other words*) now do double duty as the plain-English replacement for a banned elaboration colon.
- **Almost never:** *Furthermore*, *Moreover*, *Additionally*, *Firstly/Secondly/Thirdly*, *In conclusion*, *To summarize*, *By contrast* (rare), *Indeed* (rare), *As a result* (opener), *On the other hand*, *With that being said*, *Another important factor to consider is*, *Let's take a closer look at*. These are the connectives of generated prose (AI-detection researchers list several of them by name as the "safe statistical defaults" a model reaches for in every register); he essentially does not use them. Where the underlying logic is needed, use *Thus*, *So*, *However*, or just start the next sentence.
- **"Or, as..." attribution move:** he introduces supporting authority mid-flow, as in "Or, as John McKay... says, 'Values Matter.'"

### 1.3 Thought process (the argument pattern)

Ben reasons on the page in a repeatable five-move pattern. Use it for any analytical or persuasive passage:

1. **Open with a concession, question, or common ground**, such as "Presuming for argumentative purposes that Stephen Hawking was... correct..." or a direct question to the reader.
2. **Pivot with *However* / *Yet* / *Nonetheless***, complicating the opening.
3. **Build the case through a *For example* chain**: named authorities, concrete instances, and short quoted fragments as evidence anchors. He quotes constantly (221 blockquotes in the book, plus dozens of inline "X said," / "X wrote," attributions). Anchor claims in attributable voices rather than bare assertion.
4. **Synthesize with *Thus* / *Therefore* / *In this way***, pulling the threads together explicitly.
5. **Land on an *ought***, ending with a prescription addressed to the reader, such as "you ought to..." or "an organization must....". His writing always moves toward what the reader should *do*.

### 1.4 Person and address

- **Direct second person, heavily** (1,157 *you* in the corpus). He talks to the reader, not about an abstract audience: "you lean metaphysically," "keep in mind," "as you can see."
- **Prescriptive modality.** *Ought* is his signature modal (431 uses, as in "you ought to" or "an organization ought to lean toward..."), supported by *may* (397), *must* (171), and *would* (172). He advises and directs rather than merely describing.
- **Measured first person** for intellectual ownership: *I propose*, *I suggest*, *I believe*, *I hope*, *I would add*, *the bracketed additions are my own of course*.
- **Occasional *we/our*** when bringing the reader along, as in "as we go along" or "let's look at."

### 1.5 Vocabulary register

- **Educated-standard register with comfortable abstraction**, philosophical, ontological, metaphysical, normative, pragmatic, but always tethered to a concrete example within a sentence or two. Abstraction is never left floating.
- **Coined compound terms, written without hyphens**, as working vocabulary: *true north value*, *end goal*, *truth value*, *self organizing*, *open ended*. When a concept recurs, he names it and reuses the name consistently. Do this in any domain: coin the term once, define it, then lean on it, without joining the coinage with a hyphen.
- **Latin and foreign terms italicized with translation**: *qua*, *sui generis*, *a priori*, *Kaizen* (改善), *vis-à-vis* (this one fixed foreign phrase conventionally keeps its hyphen; do not extend that exception elsewhere). He uses *qua* heavily (295 times) where a lawyer might use "as." Use these where they carry precision, always glossed on first use.
- **"and/or"** without embarrassment (160 uses). It is precise for legal and business writing, and he treats it as standard.
- **"Generally"** (170 uses) is his preferred hedge of generality. He never writes *arguably*, *presumably*, *typically*, or *seldom*.

### 1.6 Structure and paragraph architecture

- **Short paragraphs, one idea each** (median 2 sentences). He does not wall-of-text.
- **Signposted headers** that ask questions or name the move, such as "Why Leanism?" or "Getting Around 'Leanism'". No emoji as header markers or list bullets, ever; that habit belongs to chatbot output formatting, not to Ben's writing.
- **Numbered, bold-led enumerations** when listing reasons: "**1) *Consistently Reach Higher Profits.*** You can help..." Bold the head, then explain in flowing prose. He does not use bare bullet fragments; every list item gets full sentences.
- **Quotes as structural anchors**: epigraphs and blockquotes open sections, and inline quotes close arguments.
- **Socratic questions in display position**, a standalone question to the reader, italicized or block-set, marking a turn in the argument, such as "If you have little data..., how do you... analyze true north value?"
- **Rhetorical questions throughout** (53 in the book) to implicate the reader, such as "If philosophy is dead, why not put it to practical use...?"

### 1.7 Voice calibration by deliverable

- **Legal memo / client letter (formal):** keep the rhythm, transitions, *ought*-prescriptions, and evidence-anchoring; drop the coinages, rhetorical questions, and exclamations; use Bluebook citations (Part 3) and the firm's concise-deliverable conventions (lead with questions presented and short answers). The no-dash, restricted colon/semicolon, and no-"not X, but Y" rules apply here at full strength, since legal writing is where those tics are most tempting to overuse.
- **Email / professional correspondence:** keep the direct address and short paragraphs; compress the long sentences; one *Thus*-landing max per email; sign off per standing instruction. Never open with "Great question" or close with "I hope this helps" or "Let me know if you'd like me to..."; see 2.1.
- **Blog post / thought leadership (his natural register):** full voice: questions, coinages, quotes, and *you* throughout, including the five-move argument pattern. Dashes are still off the table, colons/semicolons still need a genuine series to justify them, and the "not X, but Y" contrast is still banned; the informal-register exception that used to allow looser habits here is gone.

## Part 2 — The Anti-AI Module (never sound generated)

These patterns mark machine prose. They are banned in Ben's writing. When editing any draft (his or the agent's), strip them on sight. A word like "leverage" or "holistic" can appear in his own usage where it is earned and precise; the ban targets their reflexive, decorative use. The goal is a draft with zero surviving artifacts of AI writing: no banned phrase, no banned structural habit, no dash, no hyphen-as-punctuation, no colon or semicolon outside a genuine extended series, and no "not X, but Y" juxtaposition. This part draws on both the original corpus analysis of Ben's book and outside research into what actually gives AI-generated prose away, including Wired's July 2026 report on the "anti-AI literary counterculture" (writers deliberately avoiding em dashes and uniform sentence length to read as human) and independent analyses of ChatGPT's recurring vocabulary, sentence starters, and syntactic tics.

### 2.1 Banned words and phrases

- *delve (into)*, *tapestry*, *rich tapestry*, *multifaceted*, *plethora*, *myriad*, *cornucopia*, *symphony*, *dance of*, *realm*, *meticulous*, *boast(s)* (as in "boasts an impressive..."), *glimpse into*
- *In today's fast-paced world*, *In today's world*, *In an increasingly X world*, *In the ever-evolving landscape of*
- *It is important to note*, *It's worth noting*, *It should be noted*, *Notably,*, *It's worth mentioning*, *In many cases*, *To some extent* (as a reflexive hedge)
- *navigate the complexities of*, *shed light on*, *underscore(s)*, *highlight(s) the importance of*, *this underscores the need for*, *presents the key challenge*
- *pivotal*, *crucial role*, *plays a crucial role*, *game-changer*, *paradigm shift* (unless literal), *cutting-edge*, *state-of-the-art*, *revolutionize*, *groundbreaking*
- *testament to*, *a testament to*, *stands as a testament*
- *elevate*, *unlock*, *unleash*, *harness the power of*, *embark on a journey*, *journey* (metaphorical)
- *seamless(ly)*, *robust* (unless engineering), *comprehensive* (as filler), *powerful* (as filler), *holistic* (unless literal), *leverage* as a verb (use *use*), *utilize*, *enhances*, *facilitates*, *streamlines* (use *use*, *helps*, *improves*, *speeds up*, or name the actual mechanism instead of any of these five reflexive corporate verbs)
- *furthermore*, *moreover*, *additionally* as sentence openers (use *Thus / However / For example / Likewise* instead; see 1.2 for the fuller banned-transition list)
- *Firstly, Secondly, Thirdly* (use *First, Second, Third*)
- *In conclusion*, *To sum up*, *To summarize*, *In summary*, *Overall,* as a closing opener (land with *Thus* or just land)
- *When it comes to...*, *At its core,...*, *In essence,...*, *At the end of the day*, *That's why X is so important* (as a reflex justification tacked on with zero supporting evidence)
- *bustling*, *vibrant*, *stark contrast*, *stark* (as a stray intensifier), *dive into*, *deep dive*, *unpack*
- *Whether you're a X or a Y...*, *Look no further*, *We've got you covered*
- Empty intensifier stacks: *truly, deeply, genuinely* before every adjective
- *I hope this email finds you well*, *I hope this message finds you well*
- **Assistant-voice sycophancy and meta-commentary**, banned outright in every deliverable: *Great question!*, *Excellent question*, *Absolutely!* as a standalone opener, *You're right to ask...*, *I hope this helps*, *Let me know if you'd like me to...*, *Would you like me to...*, and any other line that talks about the act of writing or responds to the reader as though grading their question rather than answering it. Ben never validates the premise of a question before answering it; he just answers.
- Several of the phrases above happen to contain conventional hyphenated adjectives (*fast-paced*, *ever-evolving*, *game-changer*, *cutting-edge*, *state-of-the-art*). They are banned for being AI-tell phrases regardless of punctuation; rephrase both the cliche and the hyphen away.

### 2.2 Banned structural habits

- **"Not X, but Y" juxtaposition, in any of its forms.** This is the syntactic tic that gets AI writing mocked most often, precisely because it is so mechanically reliable: "That's not X, that's Y." "It's not just X, it's Y." "Not only X but Y." "X is not merely A, it is B." A widely circulated example, generated by asking a model for an essay on kindness: "Kindness is not merely a pleasant social behavior, it is a powerful force that shapes communities, improves mental and physical health, and fosters deeper human connection." Notice that the "not merely A" half does no work at all; nobody was arguing kindness is *only* a pleasant social behavior. The construction exists purely to manufacture a sense of insight through contrast. The fix is always to delete the strawman half and just assert the claim: "Kindness shapes communities, improves mental and physical health, and fosters deeper human connection." This pattern is banned even on a single use, not merely when repeated; unlike the dash or the colon, there is no legitimate exception for it. If a sentence contains a "not X, but/it's Y" shape, rewrite it as a direct positive statement.
- **Any dash, anywhere.** Em dash, en dash, and bare hyphen used as a dash are all banned in every deliverable, full stop. Worked substitutions: "The plan, which was wrong, failed." instead of a dashed aside; "The plan (which was wrong) failed." as an alternative; "pages 10 to 15" instead of a numeric-range dash; "a well known fact" instead of a hyphenated compound modifier.
- **Colon-as-elaboration and semicolon-as-clause-joiner.** A colon that introduces a single clause rather than a genuine list, and a semicolon that joins two independent clauses that could simply be split into two sentences, are both banned. This is the pattern to watch for specifically: "X is Y: [single explanatory clause]." and "[Independent clause]; [independent clause]." The worked example: "he wrote that the only truly serious philosophical problem is suicide: whether life is worth living once you've noticed it might not mean anything. His answer wasn't despair; it was defiance." becomes "he wrote that the only truly serious philosophical problem is suicide, that is, whether life is worth living once you've noticed it might not mean anything. His answer wasn't despair. It was defiance." A colon or semicolon survives review only when it is doing genuine series work (see 1.1); if you can point to the list, it stays, and if you cannot, it goes.
- **Hyphenated compound coinages**, as a lesser but related habit: prefer *true north value* over *true-north value*, *end goal* over *end-goal*. Keep the coinage; drop the hyphen.
- **Uniform sentence length (low burstiness).** Vary deliberately: long cumulative sentences punctuated by short ones (see 1.1). Three consecutive sentences of similar length and shape is a tell, and AI-detection tooling measures exactly this.
- **The rule-of-three tic.** Not every idea needs exactly three adjectives, three examples, or three bullets ("innovative, efficient, and reliable"). Use two or four when that's what the thought has. Ben's lists run long or short as needed.
- **Summary-bookend paragraphs.** Do not end a section by restating its topic sentence ("In summary, X is important."). End with a consequence, a prescription, or a question instead. In narrative or persuasive writing, resist the related habit of forcing every passage toward a tidy dramatic or rhetorical resolution, as if a conclusion were contractually required; some paragraphs can simply end.
- **Throat-clearing openings.** Never open with a definition ("X is defined as..."), a restatement of the assignment, or a scene-setter about the world's pace. Open with the concession, the question, or the claim (see 1.3).
- **Hedge-piles.** One modal is enough: *may arguably potentially suggest* is a tell. Ben hedges with a single confident *may*, *might*, or *generally*.
- **Predictable paragraph rhythm.** Statement, then explanation, then three examples, then a balanced conclusion, on a loop, is itself a tell independent of any single banned word. Break the pattern: sometimes explain before you state the claim, sometimes skip the examples, sometimes stop after two.
- **Orphan bullet fragments.** Every list item gets full sentences with a bolded head where enumerated (see 1.6). No emoji as bullets or header markers (see 1.6).
- **Emoji and exclamation padding** in professional work. Ben uses exclamation points (sparingly, about 10 in the whole book) only for genuine warmth or wit, mostly informal.
- **"As an AI..." disclosures** or robotic self-reference: never.
- **Over-formatting.** Headers on every 80-word chunk, bolding every key term, tables where prose belongs. Ben bolds enumeration heads and key coined terms only.

### 2.3 The human-alternative reflex

When a banned construction appears, replace it with the muscular plain version:

- "It is important to note that the deadline is Friday" becomes "The deadline is Friday."
- "This plays a crucial role in ensuring compliance" becomes "This keeps the company compliant." or "This ensures compliance."
- "Furthermore, the agreement provides..." becomes "The agreement also provides..." or a new sentence with *And* or *Thus*.
- "In today's fast-paced business environment, companies must adapt" becomes "Companies must adapt." Say the thing.
- "navigate the complexities of tax law" becomes "work through the tax law" or "apply the tax law."
- "The result — a stronger contract — took months" becomes "The result, a stronger contract, took months." using a comma.
- "a well-known, easy-to-apply rule" becomes "a well known, easy to apply rule."
- "he wrote that the problem is suicide: whether life is worth living. His answer wasn't despair; it was defiance." becomes "he wrote that the problem is suicide, that is, whether life is worth living. His answer wasn't despair. It was defiance." The colon and the joining semicolon are both gone; nothing about the meaning changed.
- "Kindness is not merely a pleasant social behavior, it is a powerful force that shapes communities" becomes "Kindness shapes communities." Delete the strawman half; keep the claim.
- "Great question! Here's what I'd suggest..." becomes "Here's what I'd suggest..." Just answer it.

### 2.4 Clarity cross-check: ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English

For any sentence that could be read two ways, or any paragraph that piles clauses into one long sentence, apply the discipline of ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (https://www.asd-ste100.org/), the international controlled-language standard for technical documentation: one idea per sentence, one meaning per word, the common approved word over the decorative synonym, active voice, conditions and warnings before the action. STE is a cross-check on clarity, not a register; Ben's voice still governs tone and rhythm.

Full guidance is in references/asd-ste100.md, which now carries the complete working apparatus adapted from the open-source asd-ste100 skill (https://github.com/danyuchn/asd-ste100-skill, MIT): the two modes (Strict for instructions and agent-facing text, STE-flavored for prose), the structural and lexical rule tables, the six-habit scan checklist (synonym rotation, hedge stacking, nominalization, marketing adjectives, run-on sentences, soft phrasal verbs), the rewrite process, and the output format. A deterministic linter for the structural rules ships at scripts/ste_lint.py; run it as a mechanical first pass on any draft where ambiguity has a cost. It never flags hedges or modality, so it is safe to run on legal prose.

## Part 3 — Bluebook Citation Reference (22nd ed.)

Use for legal deliverables when citation is needed. Core rules below; the full online edition (legalbluebook.com) governs when a point is not covered. **Bluepages** = practitioner/court-document style; **Whitepages** = law-review/academic style. Default to Bluepages for memos, briefs, and client work unless told otherwise. Note that citation sentences themselves (case cites, statutory strings) follow Bluebook's own colon and semicolon conventions (see 3.8 for signal strings), which are a genuine exception granted by the rules of that system, not a loophole in the prose rule above.

### 3.1 Cases (R10 / B10)

- Full cite: *Case Name*, Volume Reporter Page (Court Year). Example: *United States v. Windsor*, 570 U.S. 744 (2013).
- Italicize (or underline) case names in textual sentences and citations. Shorten long names per R10.2.
- Subsequent history and explanatory parentheticals follow the cite; use *aff'd*, *rev'd*, *cert. denied* as applicable.
- Short form: *Windsor*, 570 U.S. at 759, or *Id.* at 760 (only when immediately preceding cite is same source).
- Pincites always: cite the exact page supporting the proposition.
- Parallel citations only where required by local rule.

### 3.2 Statutes (R12 / B12)

- U.S. Code: 26 U.S.C. § 501(c)(3) (2018). Name the code, title, section symbol (§, §§ for multiple), year.
- State codes follow their official formats (e.g., N.Y. Gen. Bus. Law § 349 (McKinney 2020)).
- Session laws: cite *Stat.* when code unavailable.
- Short form: § 501(c)(3) or *id.* as appropriate.

### 3.3 Constitutions (R11)

- U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3; U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. No year for provisions in force; parenthetical year if repealed/amended.

### 3.4 Regulations (R14)

- 26 C.F.R. § 1.501(c)(3)-1 (2023). Federal Register for proposed/final rules: 88 Fed. Reg. 12,345 (Mar. 1, 2023).

### 3.5 Books (R15)

- Author, *Title* page (edition Publisher Year). Example: Bryan A. Garner, *The Chicago Guide to Grammar, Usage, and Punctuation* 55 (Univ. of Chi. Press 2016). Short: Garner, *supra* note 3, at 60.

### 3.6 Law review / periodical articles (R16)

- Author, *Title*, Volume Abbreviated Journal FirstPage, Pincite (Year). Example: John F. Coverdale, *Textualism's Gaze*, 85 Fordham L. Rev. 2029, 2035 (2017). Journal abbreviations per T13/T6.

### 3.7 Internet sources (R18)

- Author, *Title*, Site Name (last visited Date), URL. Archive URLs where possible. *Last visited* parentheticals required for undated content.

### 3.8 Signals (R1.2)

- **No signal**: the authority directly states the proposition.
- ***See***: the authority clearly supports but does not directly state.
- ***See also***: additional supporting authority.
- ***Cf.***: analogous support; comparison illuminates.
- ***Compare ... with ...***: juxtaposition is instructive.
- ***Contra***: direct contradiction.
- ***But see***: clearly contradicts or reaches a different result.
- ***But cf.***: analogously contrary.
- ***See generally***: helpful background.
- Order of signals and authorities per R1.3 to R1.4; string cites separated by semicolons per Bluebook convention (a genuine exception; citation strings are the paradigm case of an extended series); parentheticals explain relevance when not obvious.

### 3.9 Short forms and *id.* (R4)

- *Id.* only when the immediately preceding citation contains but one authority; *id.* at page for different pincite.
- *Supra* for books, articles, and previously cited non-case/non-statute material; never for cases, statutes, constitutions (use short-form case/statute rules instead).
- Do not use *infra* in court documents; use "below" in text.

### 3.10 Abbreviations and mechanics

- Case-name abbreviations per T6/T10 (e.g., *Ass'n*, *Corp.*, *Nat'l*); geographical terms per T10.
- Capitalization per R8; court and document abbreviations per B7/T1.
- Quotations: alterations bracketed (R5), omissions by ellipsis, emphasis added/omitted noted parenthetically, [sic] sparingly.
- Footnotes in academic work; in-text or footnote citations in court documents per forum practice.

### 3.11 Tax and transactional practice forms (R14, R12, R21)

The citation forms Ben actually uses. Internal revenue materials follow R14's administrative-authority conventions with tax-specific sources and abbreviations. Citation forms below use hyphens only where Bluebook mechanics require them as part of a code or case number, such as "1.368-2(b)"; that is a fixed citation format, not prose, and is not subject to the no-hyphen prose rule.

**Treasury regulations (final / temporary / proposed):**

- Final: 26 C.F.R. § 1.368-2(b) (2023). C.F.R. title equals I.R.C. title for income-tax regs.
- Temporary: 26 C.F.R. § 1.469-5T(a) (2022). The "T" suffix marks temporary; note the promulgating T.D. on first cite if relevant: T.D. 10001, 89 Fed. Reg. 84,672 (Oct. 22, 2024).
- Proposed: Prop. Treas. Reg. § 1.163(j)-1, 83 Fed. Reg. 67,490 (Dec. 28, 2018).
- In tax practice writing, "Treas. Reg. §" is standard shorthand after first full cite; in court documents use C.F.R. per Bluepages.

**Internal Revenue Code (R12.9.1):**

- I.R.C. § 368(a)(1)(A) (2018). In tax memoranda and articles, "I.R.C. §" replaces "26 U.S.C. §" per tax convention; use 26 U.S.C. § in court filings.
- Subsection chains run without spaces: § 704(b)(2)(B)(ii).

**Revenue rulings:**

- Rev. Rul. 59-60, 1959-1 C.B. 237. Pre-2000 rulings cite the Cumulative Bulletin.

## Part 4 — Garner / Chicago Grammar Principles

From Bryan A. Garner, *The Chicago Guide to Grammar, Usage, and Punctuation* (Univ. of Chi. Press 2016). These principles sit underneath all of the above.

### 4.1 Standard literary English

- Write in the forms that mark educated usage; depart from them only deliberately.
- Usage questions are empirical: prefer what careful writers actually do (Garner's Ngram method) over superstition rules. There is no rule against splitting an infinitive, ending with a preposition, or starting with *And* or *But*. Ben does all three naturally; keep them where they serve rhythm.
- Diction: prefer the precise word over the fancy one; avoid jargon where plain English carries the same meaning; keep Latinate abstraction only where it earns its place (Ben's philosophical vocabulary does; see 1.5).

### 4.2 Sentence structure and syntax

- Subject and verb close together; keep modifiers adjacent to what they modify; avoid squinting modifiers.
- Parallel structure in lists and correlative pairs (*both/and*, *either/or*, *not only/but also*) is fine when the pair is genuinely doing comparative work; it becomes the banned "not X, but Y" tic (see 2.2) the moment the first half exists only to be knocked down.
- One thought per sentence unit. Long sentences are fine when cumulative and controlled (Ben's signature), not when tangled.
- Vary openings: Ben opens with adverbs (*Thus, However*), prepositional phrases, and clauses, never a monotonous subject-first march.
- Active voice default; passive where the actor is unknown, unimportant, or tactfully omitted (lawyers know when).

### 4.3 Usage watchpoints (Garner's classic confusables)

- *comprise* vs. *compose* (the whole comprises the parts)
- *beg the question* (assumes the conclusion) vs. *raise the question*
- *e.g.* (for example) vs. *i.e.* (that is): Ben uses both precisely and never interchanges them
- *fewer* (countable) vs. *less* (mass); *amount* vs. *number*
- *who/whom* per case; *which* (nonrestrictive, comma) vs. *that* (restrictive, no comma)
- *imply* (speaker) vs. *infer* (listener); *cite/site/sight*; *principal/principle*; *affect/effect*
- *due to* as adjective only; *prior to* should usually become *before*; *whether or not* usually just needs *whether*
- Singular *they* is acceptable where recasting is awkward; consistency over dogma

### 4.4 Punctuation (Garner's punctuation part, as qualified by the standing rules)

- **Comma:** set off nonrestrictives and introductory elements; no comma between subject and verb; serial (Oxford) comma always in professional/legal writing. Ben uses it, and with dashes, colons, and clause-joining semicolons all restricted, the comma (plus period, plus subordinating conjunctions) now carries most of the connective load that this skill previously assigned to punctuation marks.
- **Semicolon:** restricted to joining three or more items in a complex series that already contains internal commas. Not used to join two independent clauses in place of a period; that use is banned outright (see 1.1 and 2.2).
- **Colon:** restricted to introducing a genuine list or enumeration of two or more parallel items. Not used to introduce a single explanatory clause, a single quoted fragment, or a single-item payoff; that use is banned outright (see 1.1 and 2.2). Where a single-item payoff is wanted, use a period and a new sentence, or the transition *that is* / *namely*, in place of the colon.
- **Dashes and hyphens-as-dashes:** not used, anywhere, under any circumstance. See 1.1 and 2.2 for the rule and the substitutions.
- **Parentheses:** de-emphasized asides and citations; never stacked more than one deep. This is now the primary tool for a mid-sentence interruption that might once have used a dash or a colon.
- **Apostrophes:** possessives per Chicago (singular *s's*: *Snipes's*); no apostrophe in plurals.
- **Quotation marks:** periods and commas inside closing quotes (American style); colons and semicolons outside (when a colon or semicolon is actually justified under the rule above); single quotes for quotes within quotes.
- **Ellipses:** three dots with spaces for omissions in quotations (align with Bluebook R5).

### 4.5 The Garner-Ben intersection

Garner's prescription and Ben's practice converge: educated-standard grammar, confident long sentences with controlled subordination, punctuation as logic rather than decoration, no superstition rules, and diction that is precise before it is impressive. When in doubt, ask five questions: would a careful lawyer write it this way; would Ben; does it contain a single dash or a hyphen doing a dash's job; does every colon or semicolon in it point to an actual, countable list; and does any sentence knock down a strawman with "not X, but Y" before making its actual point. If any answer breaks the rule, fix it before moving on.

## Part 5 — Learned from Ben's edits (calibration from his revisions)

Lessons extracted from Ben's own edits to a privileged legal memo (GDPR miner/sub-processor analysis, Aug 2026). These are observed deltas between the draft he received and the text he shipped; treat them as calibration data, weighted above the generic guide when they conflict.

### 5.1 Cut the throat-clearing harder than you think

- He deleted "It is stronger than that" and similar meta-commentary about the argument's own architecture. State the position; do not narrate how strong it is.
- He deleted "The weaknesses deserve honest statement." Just state the weaknesses. Announcing candor is a tell.
- He deleted "The weak version of your instinct therefore fails." Do not grade the reader's framing; answer the question.
- He deleted the entire "Alternative characterizations, ranked" section (independent controller, mere conduit, sole processor, protocol-as-processor). When he has already settled the ranking, he does not walk the reader through the rejected options at length. One line in the bottom line carries the alternative; the memo body covers only the live theory and the chosen fallback.

### 5.2 Substance edits that are also style

- "The real theory" became "The main theory." Prefer plain adjectives over emphatic ones.
- "Belt and braces: build Article 28 in by design anyway" became "Build Article 28 in by design." No idiomatic flourish in headings; headings are labels.
- "There is no enforcement precedent against this theory, and there is also none blessing it" became "There is no enforcement precedent against this theory, or any approving it." Compress parallel clauses into one.
- Risk ratings rendered as "Theory -> Medium." on their own line, with the reasoning in a following paragraph. He likes the arrow form for ratings; keep it.
- He names the client where the draft said "the position": "materially improves Chutes' position," "not accepting Chutes' given theory." Use the client name, not abstractions.
- He reframed the risk driver from "customer diligence and complaints, not supervisory authority initiative" to "The largest risk driver for Chutes is B2B counterparties not accepting Chutes' given theory." Lead with the commercial failure mode, name the counterparty, then the regulatory point.
- Recommendation compressed from a three-part series to one combined sentence: "Adopt the combined position that miners never hold personal data, resting on the TEE plus key custody plus relay hygiene, while Chutes develops the on-chain network terms as the Article 28 fallback." One sentence, one instruction, one follow-up action.
- He dropped the "Reliance limits" section entirely from this memo. Do not append reliance boilerplate to internal privileged memos unless asked.

### 5.3 Citations

- He adds a working URL to the case cite inline: "Breyer v. Germany (CJEU, C-582/14) https://gdprhub.eu/..." For internal memos, give him the citation plus a clickable source link, not a formatted Bluebook pin cite.

### 5.4 What he kept (do not "fix" these)

- Long cumulative sentences carried by "and," "which," "because," "while."
- The four-beat cadence "No personal data, no processor, no Article 28, no identity problem."
- "we should not pretend otherwise" — first person plural for the firm's posture is his voice.
- Section numbering gaps (his memo jumps from 3 to 5 after he deleted a section). Do not renumber his edits; ship what he ships.

### 5.5 Professional memo architecture (learned from a Perplexity deep-research memo Ben supplied as a model)

Ben shared a full-length memo on the same GDPR miner question as an example of professional memo craft. Learn from its structure and its moves; do not copy its subject matter into unrelated work.

**Skeleton for a formal legal memo.** Roman-numeral sections in this order: I. Question Presented, II. Short Answer, III. Relevant Legal Framework (lettered subsections A through F, one authority or one factual record per subsection), IV. Analysis (the arguments, layered), V. Recommendations, VI. Conclusion. Header block before Section I: title line, "Prepared for the [client] legal file. Attorney work product. Privileged and confidential." and "Prepared [date]."

**Question Presented is one long "Whether" sentence.** It defines every actor and instrument in a parenthetical on first use ("miners (the independent compute providers who supply GPU/TEE capacity...)"), states the full practical consequence of a yes answer ("such that Chutes would be required to identify each miner by name and legal entity, obtain controller authorization..."), and ends with the specific sub-question ("and, specifically, whether the fact that... changes that analysis"). One sentence, however long it runs.

**Short Answer concedes the losing point first, then pivots on "However."** Structure: (1) state what the architecture does NOT accomplish, with the adverse authority named ("per the UK Information Commissioner's Office's published position that operations on encrypted data still constitute 'processing'"); (2) "However, there is a legally defensible, evidence dependent argument that..."; (3) one sentence on what the winning argument requires; (4) one sentence on its status ("not yet been squarely adopted by the EDPB or a supervisory authority"); (5) one sentence on what the memo recommends. The whole answer in one paragraph.

**Framework before analysis, and quote the operative text.** Each framework subsection does one job: quote the statute verbatim (Article 4(2), Article 4(8)), state the elements as a numbered or lettered list ("three cumulative factual elements: (i)... (ii)... (iii)..."), then give the regulator's gloss in the regulator's own words. Never paraphrase a definition you could quote.

**Name and frame the adverse authority before the counterparty does.** The memo leads Section III.B with the ICO guidance that forecloses the broadest version of the client's theory, and says so plainly: "This guidance forecloses the broadest version of the TEE argument." Then it re-aims the analysis at the surviving question: "The correct analytical target, therefore, is not whether 'processing' occurs (it almost certainly does), but whether the miner processes 'on behalf of' the controller." Concede precisely, then relocate the fight.

**Layer the arguments so each survives the failure of the one above it.** Analysis section runs Layer One (strongest: no access to intelligible content, courier analogy), Layer Two (fallback: sufficient guarantees satisfied at the category level), Layer Three (independent of classification: contractual risk allocation). State the layering logic explicitly: "so that if a regulator or court rejects one layer, the remaining layers still narrow Chutes' exposure."

**Enumerate the winning argument's preconditions as a Roman-numeral list of technical facts.** "(i) end to end encryption... (ii) ephemeral, session bound encryption keys... (iii) remote attestation... (iv) hardware memory encryption..." The legal conclusion is then stated as following from those facts: an entity that "(a) never has a technically or practically feasible path to intelligible personal data, (b) cannot exfiltrate, retain, or repurpose that data..., and (c) performs no independent decision making" is not a processor. Facts first, conclusion second, every time.

**Disclose the argument's limitations in a numbered candor passage.** "This argument has three material limitations that must be disclosed candidly. First,... Second,... Third,..." Include the kill conditions: "If miner operators have any of the following, the argument fails: retained access to unencrypted checkpoints or logs, the ability to disable or downgrade attestation, side channel exposure..., custody of any decryption key." A memo that does not list its own defeat conditions is advocacy, not counsel.

**Track the live regulatory docket and name it.** The memo identifies the Swedish IMY TEE project by authority, docket number (IMY-2026-5444), and date, calls it "the single most relevant piece of persuasive regulatory authority," and instructs both to monitor it and not to rely on an assumed favorable outcome "in any customer facing representation, DPA clause, or public statement." When the law is unsettled, find the open file and cite it.

**Read the client's own public documents against the theory.** Section III.F pulls the client's actual posted DPA, quotes it ("We may engage sub-processors..."), and shows the exposure hiding in the client's own drafting: "As drafted, the DPA's broad 'We may engage sub-processors' language, if read literally against the miner layer, would treat the entire miner population as 'our current sub-processors.'" Always check what the client has already published.

**Recommendations are imperative sentences, one per paragraph, each with an owner-grade action.** Commission an independent technical assessment. Revise the DPA to distinguish the miner layer from conventional named subprocessors. Do not represent the theory as settled law; "represent instead, accurately, that..." Address network layer metadata separately. Prepare the fallback compliance path. Consider seeking an informal steer from a supervisory authority. No bullets with fragments; each recommendation is a full sentence that says what to do and why.

**Conclusion restates the concession and the narrow win in two sentences.** "No EDPB guideline or CJEU decision holds that TEE based execution categorically removes a compute provider from the processor/subprocessor framework... The stronger and more defensible position is narrower: ..." End on the pairing instruction: the argument "should be paired with the fallback compliance architecture... so that Chutes' regulatory position does not depend entirely on prevailing on a novel legal theory."

**Register.** Formal memos still use first person plural: "in our judgment," "we recommend," "we could not confirm." Never "in counsel's professional judgment" or "to counsel's knowledge" — standing rule 3 bans third-person self-reference in every deliverable, formal or informal. Match depth and structure to the artifact, not the person: the formal memo gets the full Roman-numeral skeleton, the internal strategy note gets the compressed version, and both speak as "we."

## Redlining Word Documents

When a deliverable is a redline or markup of a .docx, the `docx-redlining` skill governs. Mark only the words that change, never strike and retype a whole paragraph for a small edit, and always ask the user in whose name the redlines should be made before editing.

## Workflow — applying the skill

1. **Draft** with the voice guide: pick the deliverable's calibration (1.7), reason through the five-move pattern where analytical (1.3), write with Ben's sentence rhythm and transitions (1.1 to 1.2), and write it from the start with no dashes, no hyphens-as-punctuation, no colon or semicolon unless you can point to the actual list or series it serves, and no "not X, but Y" contrast, rather than adding these habits and stripping them out later.
2. **Sweep** the draft against the Anti-AI module (Part 2). Run `style_check.py` (below) on any draft text to catch tells mechanically, then fix by hand with the human-alternative reflex (2.3). Where ambiguity has a cost (instructions, agent-facing text, operative contract language), also run `scripts/ste_lint.py` and apply the STE cross-check in 2.4.
3. **Cite** per Bluebook (Part 3) when the deliverable is legal; check formats with `bluebook_ref.py`. Citation-format hyphens inside case/section numbers, and semicolons inside citation strings, are exempt from the prose rules; see 3.8 and 3.11.
4. **Polish** per Garner (Part 4): usage watchpoints, punctuation logic, Oxford comma, quotes style.
5. **Verify** no machine-tell survived: no banned word, no assistant-voice sycophancy, no uniform rhythm, no throat-clearing opener, no summary-bookend closer, no "not X, but Y" contrast, zero em dashes, zero en dashes, zero hyphens used as punctuation, and, for every remaining colon or semicolon, a genuine multi-item list or series it is doing work for. Search the draft for "—", "–", " - ", ":", ";", and "not " followed within a few words by "but" or a comma-plus-restatement, as a final mechanical check, and justify or delete each hit. Read the final aloud once; it should sound like a person who thinks for a living, not a model that is performing insight through contrast.

## Helper scripts

- `**style_check.py <file|->**` — scans text for every banned AI-tell word/phrase in Part 2.1, flags structural risks (rule-of-three runs, "It is important to note", uniform sentence-length runs, hedge piles), and reports Ben-style stats (sentence-length distribution, transition usage, dashes, semicolons, colons, questions) so you can compare a draft against his fingerprint. Reads a file path or stdin. Three results now count as hard failures rather than data points: any nonzero dash count, any colon or semicolon count that exceeds the number of genuine lists/series in the piece, and any "not X, but Y" construction the script's word list happens to catch (the script was written before this rule and will not catch every instance, so a manual read for the pattern is still required).
- `**bluebook_ref.py [topic]**` — prints the quick-reference citation format for a topic. General: `cases`, `statutes`, `constitutions`, `regulations`, `books`, `articles`, `internet`, `signals`, `short-forms`, `abbreviations`. Tax/transactional: `treasury-regs`, `irc`, `rev-rul`, `rev-proc`, `notices`, `plr`, `treaties`, `tax-court`. Or `all`. A memory aid, not a substitute for the current edition.
- `**scripts/ste_lint.py <file|->**` — deterministic, stdlib-only linter for the ASD-STE100 structural rules (2.4): semicolons, sentence length, phrasal verbs, nominalization, noun clusters, and per-file synonym rotation. Advisory findings (passive voice, compound tenses) never fail the run; `--disable` silences named rules, `--json` gives structured output, `--selftest` verifies the build. It never flags hedges or modality, so it is safe on legal prose. Use it as the mechanical first pass of the STE cross-check, then finish by hand per references/asd-ste100.md.
